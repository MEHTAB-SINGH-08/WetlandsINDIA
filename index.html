How wetlands work

A wetland is land where water stays long enough to shape the soil and the life on it. That can be a few months a year or all year round.

Four common kinds

Marsh

Shallow, reed-filled water fed by rain and rivers. Home to herons, frogs and fish nurseries.

Mangrove

Salt-tolerant trees on coasts. They break waves and shelter young fish, and depend on a freshwater and tidal balance.

Peatland

Waterlogged soil that builds up plant remains over centuries and stores huge amounts of carbon.

Floodplain and lake

River edges that fill in the monsoon and drain afterwards, soaking up floodwater and recharging groundwater.

The water budget

Every wetland keeps a water budget. Water comes in as rain, river inflow and groundwater. It leaves by evaporation, plant use, outflow and seepage. If inflows beat outflows the water rises, and if not it falls. Rain is the input that climate change alters most directly.

The hydroperiod

Scientists call a wetland's yearly pattern of rising and falling water its hydroperiod. Plants germinate, fish spawn and birds arrive in step with it. In a steady climate the curve is smooth. In a changing one, dry gaps and sudden spikes break it up.

Why rain patterns are shifting

Warmer air holds about 7% more water vapour per degree Celsius, so when rain does fall it tends to be heavier. Changing winds and ocean temperatures also move the start, end and strength of rainy seasons. For monsoon regions like India, studies point to more extreme downpours alongside uneven spells, so wetlands can get too little water for weeks and then too much in a day.

Why we should care

Wetlands act as natural sponges, buffering floods and releasing water in dry months. They filter pollutants, store carbon (peatlands cover about 3% of land yet are widely estimated to hold around a third of soil carbon), shelter wildlife and support fishing and farming for billions of people.

What changing rain does

Too little, too much, too early, too late. Each pattern harms wetlands in a different way.

Drying

Longer dry spells shrink open water. Fish are trapped in shrinking pools, amphibians lose breeding sites, and birds lose feeding grounds. Dried peat can catch fire and releases stored carbon, which adds to warming. Plants that need wet roots die back and weedy dryland species move in.

Flooding

Natural wetlands depend on regular flooding, but sudden surges are different. They drown ground-nesting birds, uproot vegetation and flush in silt, fertiliser and sewage that cause algal blooms. Fast runoff from paved or drained land also arrives all at once instead of soaking in.

Mistimed seasons

Many species are tuned to the calendar. If the water arrives late or leaves early, migratory birds can reach a wetland that is not ready, and fish may spawn after the shallows have dried.

Salt, acid and carbon

Less freshwater lets seawater push into coastal marshes and mangroves, killing salt-sensitive plants. When drained or dried, some coastal soils become acidic and peat releases carbon. A wetland that stored carbon can become a source of it.

Who feels it

Fishers lose catch, farmers lose irrigation and groundwater recharge, and coastal towns lose natural storm protection. Wetland losses show up in local food and water security long before they show up in national statistics.

The money gap

Wetlands receive less than 9% of climate finance earmarked for nature-based solutions. The report estimates that about 5.5 million square kilometres, roughly half the size of Canada, need conservation or restoration to meet global climate and biodiversity goals. Overall biodiversity funding is only about 0.25% of global GDP.

Four pathways to reverse loss

  • Plan for wetlandsPut wetlands into national and local planning.
  • Fund themCount wetlands in climate and biodiversity finance.
  • Respect their role in the water cycleTreat them as water infrastructure.
  • Blend public and private moneyScale up protection and restoration.

An older assessment quoted 35% loss of natural wetlands. The 2025 outlook reports 22% using a different method, so the numbers should not be compared directly.

Wetlands in India

India depends on a monsoon that is becoming more uneven, which makes its wetlands a close test of what changing rain does.

231,195wetlands (2.25 ha or larger) mapped in 2017-18 by ISRO
4.86%of India's land area, about 15.28 million hectares
91Ramsar sites, with Khichan and Menar added in 2025

What the government atlas says

The National Decadal Wetlands Change Atlas, released by the Environment Ministry in 2022, compares 2017-18 with 2006-07. It found that the number and area of mapped wetlands increased, but researchers warn that this includes many man-made tanks and reservoirs, and that natural wetlands are degrading. The atlas itself records declines in intertidal mudflats (4.8%), salt marshes (3.7%) and waterlogged areas (2.4%), and 1,342 wetlands that had disappeared.

Sites under strain

Keoladeo National Park in Rajasthan, India's first Ramsar site, depends on monsoon flow and managed water supply. Keoladeo and Loktak Lake in Manipur are both on the Montreux Record, Ramsar's list of sites facing ecological change. Sites like these show how a weaker or later monsoon quickly becomes a management problem.

Laws and schemes

  1. 1982. India joins the Ramsar Convention and begins designating sites.
  2. 2015. The National Plan for Conservation of Aquatic Ecosystems merges two earlier lake and wetland programmes.
  3. 2017. Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules set up a national committee and state wetland authorities.
  4. 2022. The Decadal Change Atlas is released on World Wetlands Day.
  5. 2023. Amrit Dharohar launches, focusing on Ramsar sites through community participation, eco-tourism and local livelihoods.
  6. 2025. India reaches 91 Ramsar sites.

The gaps

The 2017 Rules apply to wetlands notified by state governments, and do not cover rivers. Court orders have urged a wider approach, treating wetlands in the ISRO atlas as water bodies that need protection. Enforcement and state-level capacity remain the main challenge.

What can be done

We cannot control the rain, but we can make wetlands better able to cope with it.

  • Keep water connectedProtect inflow channels, floodplains and groundwater so wetlands can fill and drain naturally.
  • Re-wet and replantRestore drained peat, replant mangroves and rebuild buffer strips. Healthy wetlands soak up floods and hold water through dry spells.
  • Manage dams and canals for wetlandsRelease water on schedules that match birds and fish, not just farms and cities.
  • Stronger law and local actionEmpower state wetland authorities, include rivers and unnotified wetlands, and involve communities through schemes like Amrit Dharohar.
  • Monitor earlyCombine satellite wetland maps with rain-gauge and water-level data to spot stress while action is still cheap.
  • Finance and planBuild wetlands into climate, land-use and urban plans and mix public and private funding.

What one person can do

Support local lake and wetland cleanups, avoid dumping waste or filling in ponds, harvest rainwater so less runoff reaches wetlands, and ask your local government whether nearby wetlands are notified and protected.
